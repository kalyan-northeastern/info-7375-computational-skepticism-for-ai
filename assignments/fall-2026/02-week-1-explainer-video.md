## Ideas that are the right size

**The four moves** — Cartesian doubt as an inspection protocol that turns a philosophical question into a checkable list · Hume's limit on induction, and why a thousand correct predictions add zero logical guarantee about the next one · the turkey problem, and the difference between knowing a correlation and knowing a mechanism · Popper's asymmetry: no number of white swans proves the claim, one black swan destroys it · the demarcation between an engineering claim and a rhetorical one · Plato's Cave — what is the artifact, what is the world, what is the relationship between them

**The Five Supervisory Capacities** — take *one*: plausibility auditing · problem formulation · tool orchestration · interpretive judgment · executive integration. For your chosen capacity, show concretely what exercising it looks like and what its absence looks like.

**The structural claims** — the solve-verify asymmetry, and why it inverts for AI · the fluency trap as a two-stage mechanism, and why fluency boosts your confidence in your *own evaluation* · why individual skepticism is fragile and institutional skepticism is architecture

**A case, worked** — the sepsis model that succeeded by every measure its builders chose · Ash's agent reporting a deletion that did not happen · Zillow Offers · Google Flu Trends and big-data hubris. If you take a case, the video's job is to name **which move or capacity would have caught it**, not to retell the story.

## What "explain it well" means

This is a course about doubting outputs, so the video is held to its own standard.

1.  **Make the idea do work.** Don't define Popperian falsifiability and stop. Take a real claim — one from your own field is best — and show it failing the test, then show the corrected version that specifies a metric, a threshold, and a window.
2.  **Use a concrete instance, not a category.** "AI can't understand context" is a category. "The model reported the email was deleted; the email was on the provider's servers" is an instance. The chapter's own AI Use Disclosure standard applies to your explanation too.
3.  **Name one thing your explanation does not establish.** Chapter 1 ends with *What would change my mind* and *Still puzzling* for a reason. Do the same.
4.  **Do not let your own video be fluent and wrong.** If you assert a fact about a case — a number, a date, an outcome — check it against the primary source, not a news summary. A polished video that misstates what the Michigan team found has demonstrated the fluency trap rather than explained it.

Point 4 is the assignment grading itself. A video that sounds authoritative and gets the case wrong has failed on the chapter's own terms, however good it looks.

The last field is graded and is not optional. Name something concrete and specific to *your* video — a judgment call that required your domain knowledge, your values, or your accountability. "AI cannot understand context" is a category claim and earns nothing.
